This is the third text in Forward Science, a series on the institutions needed to deploy AI, computation, automation, and scientific judgment.

The Forward-Deployed Scientist is easier to understand through an older problem in the organization of science. Research historically depended on institutions able to move formal knowledge into settings where people act under local constraint, incomplete information, and real costs of error. The difficulty lies in the passage from a validated claim to a claim that deserves practical authority.

Agricultural extension offers one of the clearest precedents. In the XIXth century, a university experiment station would establish that a new method improved crop performance under controlled conditions, and the finding still had to enter the practical world of the farm. The farmer encountered it through the character of his own land, the limits of his equipment, the finances of the season, and the risk attached to failure. A result that was sound in the experiment station could still require adaptation before it could alter practice with legitimacy. The extension agent occupied the institutional space between research and use. The role was neither the simple transmission of university knowledge nor the passive accommodation of local custom. It required demonstration, adaptation, persuasion, and listening, as the site of application could reveal constraints the research institution had failed to see. In its strongest form, extension treated local practice as a source of intelligence as well as a recipient of expertise.

This history is relevant to AI-age science as the old problem of mediation has moved inside the laboratory. Frontier models, automated instruments, and computational infrastructure can enter a research group without giving that group a stronger way to inquire. Their value depends on their relation to the laboratory’s evidentiary practices, practical constraints, and standards of judgment. The Forward-Deployed Scientist belongs to an older family of scientific intermediaries, although the setting has changed. The field is now the research system itself, and the central problem is how advanced capability can answer to the conditions under which scientific knowledge is produced. A familiar account of diffusion still shapes expectations about AI for science. Research institutions produce knowledge, publications circulate it, tools distribute it, and users are expected to integrate it into practice. The same expectation now attaches to frontier models, as though stronger systems and better interfaces would by themselves accelerate discovery once they reach scientists. Access is a necessary condition, especially where advanced computational systems remain unevenly distributed, yet it does not supply incorporation. A laboratory still has to determine how a tool enters its evidentiary order, how its outputs acquire standing, and how they alter the next scientific decision.

Agricultural extension also supplies an institutional warning. Deployed expertise can distribute capability, but it can also project power. Indeed, it can help a community use science more effectively, or it can impose an external model of improvement. A the same time, it can strengthen a place, or it can extract knowledge from that place while leaving little durable capacity behind. The AI age intensifies these possibilities, as tools entering laboratories can alter research practice while shifting control toward institutions that own models, platforms, or infrastructure.

Survey science offers a second genealogy for Forward-Deployed Science. Before a state, university, or scientific agency can act on a complex setting at scale, that setting must first be rendered into forms that travel. Geological surveys and field stations did much more to our collective knowledge than collect facts about terrain. They consistently produced institutional sight by translating situated environments into records, classifications, measurements, and standard descriptions. That sight could support public planning and scientific understanding, although it could also support extraction and control. The Forward-Deployed Scientist conducts a comparable survey inside the research system itself. The terrain is the laboratory’s passage from question to evidence to subsequent action. The work is diagnostic before it is constructive, as a laboratory’s initial request often identifies the most visible symptom of difficulty while leaving the operative obstruction elsewhere. To understand the research loop, the Forward-Deployed Scientist has to follow a claim through the records, instruments, procedures, assumptions, maintenance practices, and decisions that shape its trajectory.

Field epidemiology adds a different lesson, drawn from action under uncertainty. During an outbreak, evidence arrives unevenly and often too late for the comfort of perfect inference, while public action cannot wait for ideal knowledge. The field epidemiologist gathers information under constraint and helps translate incomplete evidence into decisions that can protect people. The work requires speed, while speed remains answerable to uncertainty, revision, and public accountability. Forward-Deployed Scientists inherit this posture in research settings where urgency might be less visible, although the cost of inertia can still be substantial. Many scientific loops fail in practice today as evidence accumulates without revising the next decision. Results are stored, discussed, and sometimes modeled while the laboratory continues along a path the evidence should have changed. The Forward-Deployed Scientist asks where the evidentiary chain loses force and whether the result has actually altered the next experiment, the treatment of uncertainty, the procedure, or the continuation of the line of inquiry.

Instrument science offers the closest analogy within contemporary research practice. Large facilities have long required forms of expertise that sit between a researcher’s question and an apparatus whose operation exceeds ordinary use. Access to a beamline or sequencing core has scientific value only when the inquiry is shaped into a form the instrument can answer. The instrument scientist protects that relation, ensuring that use produces defensible evidence instead of costly output. AI-age science extends this problem from a single apparatus to the research system as a whole. Computational models now operate in relation to experimental platforms, data histories, laboratory procedures, human expertise, and institutional memory. The scientific relation rests in the coordination of the system and the Forward-Deployed Scientist acts as an interface scientist for this distributed apparatus, responsible for the route through which computational output enters a valid evidentiary sequence.

Instrument science also warns against dependence. Scarce apparatus concentrates access, and specialized expertise creates gates through which other scientists must pass. Forward-Deployed Science should avoid creating a new priesthood around AI-enabled research. Its strongest form increases the laboratory’s independent capability while returning reusable lessons to the broader research system.

Research Software Engineering supplies a more recent institutional precedent. Modern science depended on code long before universities developed adequate ways to credit the people who made that code reliable. Research software shaped results through analysis pipelines, and simulation systems that often sat outside standard career structures. The Research Software Engineering movement gave institutional visibility to a form of scientific labor that had long been indispensable. That precedent shows how informal necessity can acquire professional form. Research Software Engineering demonstrated that modern research depends on people whose excellence appears through maintainability, reproducibility, collaboration, and infrastructure. It also showed that naming a role supplies only an initial condition. A profession requires community, standards, career paths, funding, evaluation, and institutional legitimacy.

Forward-Deployed Scientists will face the same problem of recognition. Much of their contribution will appear between familiar categories of academic credit. They might repair the data conditions that give an experiment meaning, redesign an interface so that results alter the next decision, or create the handoff package that lets a laboratory continue after the intervention. Such work can shape scientific truth while remaining difficult to credit inside paper-centered evaluation.

The history of scientific intermediaries is also a history of labor that institutions have often struggled to recognize. Extension, surveying, epidemiology, instrument science, and research software all show how much scientific progress depends on work performed at boundaries. Institutions tend to honor the final claim more readily than the mediation that made the claim possible. Forward-Deployed Science should resist that pattern by linking the role from the outset to explicit credit, accountability, and career development.

The Forward-Deployed Scientist belongs to an older family of scientific intermediaries, now reconstructed for a research environment in which the apparatus of discovery is distributed across computation, automation, instruments, software, governance, and human judgment. The role occupies the point at which knowledge enters action and must be made accountable to evidence, local constraint, and institutional consequence. Science has long required mediation at this point. AI has made the cost of weak mediation harder to ignore.

The next text turns from historical genealogy to institutional design. If the Forward-Deployed Scientist is a real role, science must answer a practical question about how to finance work whose value lies in making other scientists more capable.
